# ------------------------------------------------------------------ ABSTRACT
@FRONT|ABSTRACT|Abstract

@P|Bangladeshi social-media monitoring runs on text that off-the-shelf language technology handles poorly: Bangla script, English and romanized "Banglish" mix inside single comments, and the platforms supplying the data store one coarse sentiment per post and none per comment. Sending every item to a large language model is accurate but unaffordable at realistic volumes, while small classifiers alone are affordable but weak on exactly the code-mixed text that dominates the traffic. Published cost-aware cascades resolve that tension for single queries only: none routes a *thread* whose thousands of comments each need labelling, none reports what share of the bill the routing decision governs, and none carries alias-aware target stance or per-label provenance into a deployable low-resource-language system. Our objective is therefore to build, measure and audit a microservice that decides per unit of work how much intelligence that unit deserves, and can prove afterwards, from its own counters, where the money went and what produced every label.

@P|We built a five-stage, queue-based pipeline - ingestion, cheap NLP, router, selective LLM and result assembler - that emits one schema-validated JSON document per thread. A six-gate router governs post-level spend, while a separate decision selects which comments an eight-labeller ensemble reads: seven small multilingual sentiment heads batched on CPU plus one context-aware LLM stance pass, in which a labeller that cannot answer abstains rather than casting a fabricated vote. Watchlist-driven target stance adds per-entity verdicts through an alias matcher built for the three-script spelling problem, and nine tool-using agents reason over the system's own output through eighteen typed Model Context Protocol tools. Over 50 threads carrying 10,272 real comments we measured that the router sends 16 percent of posts to post-level work against 74 percent for a keyword baseline, yet governs only 30 percent of spend, because 96 percent of calls are per-comment; hybrid retrieval reaches recall@10 of 0.8750 against 0.1875 for hash-vector baselines. The conclusion this supports is narrower than the one we set out to make, and more useful: when the routed unit contains many sub-items that each need work, routing the container is not the cost lever. We deliver working, auditable software in which every capability is reported alongside the class of evidence that supports it.

@CHAPTER|1|Introduction

@INTRO|This chapter states the problem we address, the computational reasons that make it worth solving, the objectives that follow from it, and a summary of the method and the outcomes. It closes with a chapter-by-chapter map of the rest of the report.

@H2|1.1  Introduction

@P|Bangladesh has one of the largest and most conversational social-media populations in South Asia, and the organisations that monitor it - newsrooms, brand agencies, election observers, civic groups - increasingly procure that monitoring as a service. A platform of this kind scrapes public posts and their comment threads and exposes them over a REST interface, but what it typically does **not** do is understand them: the platform supplying our data stores one coarse sentiment per post, leaves per-comment sentiment empty, and ships no optical character recognition. Everything an analyst wants to know - what the thread is arguing about, which commenters are angry and at whom, whether a conversation is turning toxic - has to be computed by something else, and computing it is harder here than the English-language literature suggests, because the text is neither Bangla nor English but a live mixture written in two scripts with no standard orthography. Over the 10,272 comments in our working corpus, 81.4 percent are Bangla script only, 11.1 percent Latin only, 4.6 percent both, and 2.9 percent neither, being pure emoji.

@P|Two research gaps follow. The first is economic: sending every post and comment to a large language model works and is unaffordable, because per-token billing scales with the number of comments rather than posts, and one viral thread here carries 2,857 stored comments. The cost-aware cascade literature - FrugalGPT [30], RouteLLM [31], Hybrid LLM [32] - establishes the shape of the answer, but all three route a **single query** to a single model. None routes a container whose sub-items each need labelling, so none reports what share of the bill the container-level decision governs, and the natural reading of that literature - that the gate is the cost lever - has never been tested on this shape of unit. The second gap is linguistic: for political and brand monitoring the operative question is not whether a comment carries negative affect but which entity it is directed at. Target-dependent stance detection is established for English [13], [14], [16] and under-served for code-mixed Bangla, where the hard part is the matcher rather than the classifier, because the same entity is written three ways and a watchlist matching one spelling silently matches almost nothing - indistinguishable from an entity never discussed. Section 2.3 states these and two further gaps formally.

@P|We build a self-contained analysis microservice that answers both. Given a post with its embedded comment thread, it decides *per unit of work* how much intelligence that unit deserves, spends the expensive model only where cheap models cannot answer, and can prove afterwards from its own counters where the money went and what produced every label. It pulls from the upstream platform, analyses in its own datastores, writes nothing back, and returns one schema-validated JSON document per thread. Around that core sit the capability layers the setting demands: an eight-labeller comment ensemble with explicit abstention, watchlist-driven target stance over an alias-aware entity list, and an agentic insight layer whose tool-using agents reason over the system's own analytical output through typed Model Context Protocol tools.

@P|The contribution is threefold. First, a **measurement rather than an assertion**: the router behaves exactly as designed, sending 16 percent of posts to post-level work against 74 percent for a keyword baseline, yet governs only 30 percent of large-model calls, because between 85 and 96 percent are comment-level. That contradicted our own headline claim and replaced it with a narrower one - when the routed unit contains many sub-items, routing the container is not the cost lever. Second, **capability layers built for the code-mixed setting and reported honestly**: eight labellers per comment where one that cannot answer abstains rather than voting neutral, alias-aware target stance at zero additional model calls, and hybrid retrieval measured at recall@10 of 0.8750 against 0.1875. Third, an **evidence discipline**: we publish every capability alongside the class of evidence supporting it, so the boundary of what we evaluated is part of the result.

@H2|1.2  Motivation

@P|The computational motivation is a cost asymmetry large enough to design a system around. A small transformer classifier of 135 to 280 million parameters, batched on CPU, labels a short comment in the order of a hundred milliseconds and costs nothing per token. A seven-billion-parameter instruction-tuned model answers the same question better on code-mixed text, but a single post-type call on the local deployment we use takes between 7 and 50 seconds. The gap is two to three orders of magnitude in latency and, on a metered backend, a comparable gap in money, so a system that can decide *which* items genuinely need the expensive path converts an unbounded bill into a bounded one.

@P|That decision is not free to make well. A confidence gate is only as good as the estimate feeding it, and a confidence estimate produced by a model never calibrated on the target language is a guess wearing a number; the published cascade work establishes the shape of the answer for English but not what the gate should read when the input is code-mixed, when a post has no text, or when the routed unit is a thread of thousands of comments. The second motivation is operational: a monitoring product that reports a sentiment chart without saying which model produced each label cannot be audited, and in a political setting an unauditable label is a different kind of claim. Both point at the same commitment, and it is the one we designed around: decide selectively, and record what was decided.

@H2|1.3  Objectives

@P|Four objectives follow from the problem statement of Section 1.1, and each answers exactly one of the four gaps we identify in Section 2.3. We scoped each so that it can be checked against the running system rather than argued for, and ordered them so that the earlier are preconditions for the later.

@NUM|O1.  **Build the analysis service.** To design and implement a horizontally scalable, queue-based analysis microservice that consumes a post-with-details payload - a post with its comment thread, engagement counts, reaction breakdown and share sample embedded - from an existing monitoring platform, analyses it in its own datastores, and returns one schema-validated JSON document per thread without ever writing back to the upstream platform.
@NUM|O2.  **Make the cascade selective, and measure it.** To implement a hybrid cascade in which cheap NLP runs on every post and every comment while a rule-based router decides, per post, whether post-level large-language-model work is warranted and, as a separate decision, which comments the expensive labellers read - and then to measure that cascade rather than assert it, reporting the routing rate from the router's own counters, the split of large-model calls between the two lanes, and the share of total spend the routing gate actually governs.
@NUM|O3.  **Deliver the capability layers the code-mixed setting needs, including an agentic insight layer over the Model Context Protocol.** To raise per-comment sentiment from a single verdict to a transparent eight-labeller ensemble in which a labeller that cannot answer abstains rather than casting a fabricated neutral vote; to add watchlist-driven target stance through a configurable, alias-aware entity list yielding per-entity verdicts for Bangla, Banglish and English in a field kept separate from document sentiment; and to build an **agentic retrieval-augmented insight layer** in which tool-using agents reason over the system's own structured analytical output through **Model Context Protocol servers exposing typed tools**, under per-run tool and token budgets, prompt-injection hardening and per-claim citation verification.
@NUM|O4.  **Make every claim auditable, and evaluate against that standard.** To carry provenance on every label, per-backend and per-lane cost telemetry, a live per-post execution trace and a regression suite that fails when a repaired defect returns; and to evaluate the system on the properties by which an engineering deliverable is judged - retrieval quality, routing behaviour, call and cost splits, coverage arithmetic and system correctness - attaching an evidence class to every published claim so that the boundary of what was evaluated is part of the result.

@P|The numbering is load-bearing rather than decorative: **a gap, the objective that answers it and the outcome that reports it all carry the same number**, so any one of the four can be followed from the gap that motivated it, through Table {{tab:gaps}}, to the measurement that closes it in Section 1.5. We prioritised them in the order given - pipeline and router first, capability layers second, audit and evidence last - which is the order in which Chapter 3 develops them and Chapter 4 measures them.

@H2|1.4  Methodology

@P|We adopted an iterative methodology structured around requirement analysis, design, development, testing, deployment and audit. The six steps are summarised here and developed in Chapter 3.

@NUM|1. **Requirement analysis.** We reviewed the three relevant literatures and characterised the real payload rather than a specification of it - 50 threads, 10,272 stored comments, one coarse upstream sentiment per post and none per comment - which produced the gap analysis of Section 2.3 and the requirements of Section 3.1.3.
@NUM|2. **System design.** We architected the system as five queue-decoupled stages connected by durable streams, and designed the router as **two independent decisions** - whether a post earns post-level work, and which comments the Stage-2 labellers read. We recorded every significant decision with its alternatives in Table {{tab:alternatives}}.
@NUM|3. **Development.** We implemented the five stages, the six-gate router, the two-lane Stage 2, the eight-labeller ensemble with abstention, watchlist stance, three MCP servers with nine agents over them, and a React dashboard - 56,789 lines of Python across 182 files.
@NUM|4. **Testing.** We wrote contract tests asserting that every document crossing a boundary validates against its schema, ran six independent audit passes that each started from a claim in this report and checked it against the running code, and pinned every defect we repaired with a regression test named after it.
@NUM|5. **Deployment.** We packaged the system for three tiers - a single-host stack, a nineteen-image Compose deployment and a Kubernetes deployment with queue-depth autoscaling - made the inference backend pluggable per call, and added a tenant privacy lock.
@NUM|6. **Evaluation.** We measured system properties and retrieval quality by running the real stage functions over the real corpus, and attached an evidence class to every capability.

@P|We split the evaluation deliberately by what the evidence can support, and that split is the methodological commitment we would defend first. We measure system properties, measure retrieval quality against ground truth and pin correctness by regression; labelling accuracy is **outside our evaluation scope** and we do not claim it, because obtaining it honestly requires an annotation programme rather than a further script.

@H2|1.5  Project Outcome

@P|We delivered a working, auditable analysis microservice comprising 56,789 lines of Python across 182 files, of which 19,850 are tests, with a React dashboard, deployment manifests for Docker Compose and Kubernetes, and an evaluation harness. We report its outcomes under the numbers of Section 1.3, so each is read against the objective it answers.

@NUM|O1.  **The analysis service - delivered.** We built a five-stage, queue-decoupled microservice that ingests a post with its whole stored comment thread, analyses it in its own datastores and emits one schema-validated JSON document per thread, writing nothing back to the platform whose coarse score it preserves as a baseline. It runs in three deployment shapes from a one-command start, behind an eleven-tab dashboard over a documented 51-path API, and verifies as one pass-or-fail command in seventy-three seconds (Section 4.2.2).
@NUM|O2.  **The selective cascade, measured - delivered, with a result that contradicted our plan.** Our router sends 16 percent of posts to post-level large-model work against 74 percent for a keyword baseline, and we separated its two decisions so that comment selection can be reported apart from post-level spend. Measuring it then produced our central finding: **the gate governs only 30 percent of large-model calls, because between 85 and 96 percent of calls are comment-level** (Section 4.2.4). The routing rate is not the cost story, and no published cascade had reported that because none routes a container.
@NUM|O3.  **The capability layers, including the agentic MCP insight layer - delivered.** We raised per-comment sentiment to eight independent labellers with explicit abstention, voter counts and per-source verdicts side by side, so a comment no model read is reported as *uncertain* rather than defaulted to neutral. Watchlist-driven target stance yields per-entity verdicts over an operator-supplied, alias-aware list at **zero additional large-model calls**, in a field kept separate from document sentiment. Above them, the **agentic insight layer runs nine tool-using agents over eighteen typed tools across three Model Context Protocol servers**, hardened against six failure modes observed in live runs; its retrieval reaches recall@10 of 0.8750 where hash-seeded vectors score 0.1875 (Section 4.2.5).
@NUM|O4.  **Auditability and its evaluation - delivered.** We carry provenance on every label, meter spend per backend, model and lane, expose a live per-post trace that makes the router's decision inspectable, and pin one regression per repaired defect across a 1,420-test suite. @CHAPTER|2|Background

@INTRO|This chapter supplies the background needed to read the rest of the report. We review the state of sentiment and stance analysis for Bangla and code-mixed text, the literature on cost-aware model cascades, and the recent work on retrieval-augmented generation and tool-using agents, then compares the proposed system against existing monitoring products and identifies the gaps this project sets out to fill.

@H2|2.1  Introduction

@P|Three research literatures meet in our work: **sentiment and stance analysis for low-resource and code-mixed languages**, surveyed by Wankhade et al. [12], which supplies the models and the evidence of how badly they degrade on romanized input; **cost-aware inference**, the work on cascades and query routing that establishes the shape of a system in which a cheap model answers most queries and an expensive one answers the rest; and **retrieval-augmented generation with tool-using agents**, which supplies the pattern for the insight layer. A short orientation on each is given here; the comparison follows in Section 2.2.

@P|Modern text classification for this task rests on the transformer encoder [18] and the masked-language-model objective introduced by BERT [19]. XLM-RoBERTa [20] is the standard multilingual baseline, handling Bangla and English in one model and therefore a code-mixed sentence without a language-identification step in front of it; **BanglaBERT** [1] is the leading monolingual option. Both matter, because we deliberately mix multilingual encoders with Bangla-specific fine-tunes in the ensemble of Chapter 3: a multilingual model that merely *includes* Bangla is a different kind of evidence from one trained on it. Around those we use the supporting models - sentence embeddings from the Sentence-BERT family [22] and its multilingual distillation [23], which lets an English query retrieve a Bangla post; GLiNER [25] for entities; fastText [21] for language identification; the zero-shot formulations of Yin et al. [26]; and ModernBERT [24], included because a voter that fails differently is worth more than one that fails the same way.

@P|Code-switching is not an edge case in this corpus but the dominant register. Sitaram et al. [11] name the central difficulty: romanized text belongs to no language model's pretraining distribution and standard tokenisers fragment it badly. SemEval-2020 Task 9 [10] confirmed that the code-mixed portion lags the monolingual one substantially; Hassan et al. [3] were among the first to treat romanized Bangla as a first-class target rather than noise, and Alam et al. [9] conclude that the bottleneck is annotated data rather than architecture. One further property shapes an annotation decision in Chapter 6: many comments carry emoji, whose sentiment is neither uniform nor obvious [17].

@P|The engineering core belongs to a line of work only a few years old. FrugalGPT [30] showed that a cascade gated by a learned scorer can match a single large model's quality at a fraction of the cost; RouteLLM [31] learned the routing decision from preference data; Hybrid LLM [32] framed it as a cost-versus-quality trade with a tunable knob. All three route **queries**, none routes a *thread*, and the distinction matters: when the unit of analysis contains thousands of sub-items, the decision on the container governs a minority of the bill. On the serving side, PagedAttention and vLLM [33] make self-hosted inference economical enough for the local backend to be the default. Above the pipeline, retrieval-augmented generation [34] grounds an answer in retrieved evidence and Gao et al. [35] map the design space; quality is measured by recall@k, MRR@k and the cumulated-gain measures of [41], with reciprocal rank fusion [39] combining arms that fail on different queries and HNSW graphs [40] indexing the dense arm, while Cuconasu et al. [42] give the warning that led us to report recall rather than precision. ReAct [36] and Toolformer [37] established the tool-using loop, and the Model Context Protocol [38] standardises the tool interface along with, as Hou et al. document, a new attack surface.

@H2|3.1  Requirement Analysis and Design Specification

@H3|3.1.1  Overview

@P|We built the system as a **self-contained analysis microservice** sitting beside an existing social-media monitoring platform. Its contract is one-directional: it **pulls** a post-with-details payload, analyses it in **its own** datastores, and **never writes back**. The payload is one JSON object per post carrying the post, its embedded comment thread, engagement counts, a reaction breakdown and a sample of shares, so no second fetch is required and the natural unit of analysis is **a post together with its comment thread**.

@P|Four properties of the input govern everything downstream. The payload arrives **whole**, so the system never reasons about a partially retrieved conversation. The thread is a **sample**, not a census - here 3.75 percent of what the platform holds - so any honest output must report coverage rather than imply completeness. Records are keyed by the **upstream identifier**, making a re-pull an upsert and the ingestion path idempotent without coordination between the two systems. And the platform supplies **one coarse sentiment per post and nothing per comment**, so the per-comment lane creates a signal that does not otherwise exist. We never write anything back: a deliberate commitment, which means the system cannot corrupt the source of truth, and it preserves the platform's own score beside the recomputed one as a baseline.

@H3|3.1.2  System Design

@P|The figure is five horizontal bands, and each boundary trades something for a property the system needs. The **client band** holds two consumers we treat identically - our own dashboard and any third-party application - because both arrive through the same gateway over the same authenticated API, so no capability can exist that the dashboard has and an integrator does not. @P|Inside the **pipeline band** the router is the sole component permitted to decide how much work an item receives, which is what lets cost behaviour be reasoned about by reading one component rather than auditing five. @NUM|P1. **Hybrid intelligence, not a large model everywhere.** Small specialised models are two to three orders of magnitude cheaper and faster per item; the large model is reserved for what small models cannot do at all.
@NUM|P2. **Queue-based and stage-decoupled.** Every heavy stage is a stateless worker pool fed by a durable stream, so back-pressure, retries and independent scaling come for free.
@NUM|P3. **Scale by partition, not by bigger machines.** The bus is partitioned by a hash of the post identifier, so a post's ordering is stable while load spreads evenly.
@NUM|P4. **Idempotent and observable.** Every post has a stable content hash; every stage emits progress events, metrics and traces; and every value in the output carries provenance saying what produced it.

@H3|3.1.3  Functional and Nonfunctional Requirements

@P|These are requirements on the **software system we built**, not on the research, and we state each once with the design element that satisfies it named alongside.

@P|Eighteen functional requirements say what the system must do. It shall pull a post-with-details payload and accept a pushed batch for replay (FR1), derive the source platform from each post's URL host (FR2), and preserve the upstream sentiment and virality score as baselines it never overwrites (FR3). Over every ingested thread it shall detect language and flag romanized Banglish with the method that produced the verdict (FR4); recompute post-level sentiment, emotion, topics, intents, post type, toxicity, entities and keywords (FR5); and label every stored comment with provenance attached (FR6). It shall decide per post whether post-level large-model work is warranted (FR7) and, separately, which comments the expensive labellers read (FR8). Each analysed comment shall receive multiple independent verdicts combined by a single writer, with abstention rather than a default vote (FR9); each post a summary in its own language that recovers from truncation (FR10); and watchlist stance shall occupy a field separate from document sentiment (FR11). It shall emit one schema-validated JSON document per thread into three stores (FR12), report comment coverage clamped with an anomaly flag (FR13), and allow a job to be started, tracked, stopped, resumed and deleted (FR14). Above the pipeline it shall support identifier, keyword, semantic and hybrid retrieval (FR15), answer corpus-level questions with grounded cited answers from tool-using agents (FR16), report token and cost usage per backend, model and lane (FR17), and expose all of this through an operator dashboard (FR18).

@P|Twelve non-functional requirements state how well. Batches shall scale from one thousand to one hundred thousand threads by adding workers and partitions rather than larger machines (NFR1); the expensive model shall be spent selectively and the spend attributable afterwards (NFR2); and Bangla, English and romanized Banglish shall all be handled (NFR3). No post may be silently lost, so bounded retry carries its attempt counter inside the payload before dead-lettering (NFR4); reprocessing shall be safe, through content-hash keys, upserts and a collapsing analytics table (NFR5); and a stop shall cost at most one post per stage (NFR6). Every stage boundary shall be visible per post (NFR7), and no output may claim more than the system did, through degraded-component reporting, per-label provenance and a stub-vector flag on every embedding (NFR8). Post text shall be treated as untrusted, with token verification on every transport, hashed keys, single-use tickets and fail-closed policy checks (NFR9); a privacy-locked tenant's content shall never leave the cluster (NFR10); the same code shall run on a laptop and in a cluster (NFR11); and a cross-component rename shall break a test rather than a feature (NFR12). Every functional requirement is exercised by the full-chain run of Section 4.2.2; NFR1 and NFR6 are properties of a deployment shape Chapter 4 did not measure, and Section 4.3.3 records them as design claims.

@H2|3.2  Detailed Methodology and Design

@P|Section 3.1 covered the high-level design; this section goes into the technical decisions beneath it, each with the alternatives weighed and the constraint that settled it. Table {{tab:alternatives}} at the end of the section collects them in one place.

@H3|3.2.1  The five stages

@P|The pipeline is five stages joined by four Redis streams: ingestion writes `posts.ingested`; Stage-1 cheap NLP writes `nlp.done`; the router writes `llm.tasks`; Stage-2 writes `llm.done`; and the assembler fans out to the three stores. Every stream and consumer-group name comes from a single constant module, which exists because four separate defects here had the identical shape: one component wrote a string, another read a different string, and the mismatch degraded to a silent no-operation rather than an error. A test asserts that the autoscaler manifests name exactly the streams and groups the workers create, so a rename breaks the build instead of quietly disabling an autoscaler.

@P|Four behaviours are implemented once in a shared worker base class and therefore hold at every hop. **Consumer groups are created on start**, so replicas split the stream rather than duplicating it. **Cancellation is cooperative**: the four stages ahead of the assembler check a per-job Redis flag as they pick a message up and drop it rather than doing the work, bounding a stop at one post per stage; the assembler deliberately does not check, because a post that reached it has already had every model run on it. **Retry is bounded and then dead-letters**, with the attempt counter inside the payload rather than keyed by stream identifier, because a re-enqueued message gets a fresh identifier and a per-identifier counter would loop forever. **Progress events** are published at each hop and, because publish-subscribe has no backlog, also appended to a capped list and replayed on connect with a monotonic sequence number.

@H3|3.2.2  The router: two independent decisions

@P|The router is the component the entire cost argument rests on, and it makes **two independent decisions**: whether the post earns post-level large-model work, and which comments the Stage-2 labellers read. Conflating them was the mistake in our original design. The first is taken by the six gates of Table {{tab:gates}}, any one of which is sufficient to route, with every reason recorded as a string so the routing rate can be decomposed by cause rather than merely counted.

@TABLE|tab:gates|The six routing gates.
@TH|#|Gate|Fires when|Threshold knob (default)
@TR|1|Low or absent confidence|Stage-1 overall confidence is below the threshold, or is absent entirely|ROUTER_CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD (0.8)
@TR|2|Post type unknown or weak|The semantic post type is unset, or its confidence is below the threshold|ROUTER_POST_TYPE_CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD (0.8)
@TR|3|Caller asked for a Stage-2 summary|The request explicitly asks for the better summary model|ROUTER_SUMMARY_ROUTES (false)
@TR|4|Image post with no image verdict|The post carries photographs but the vision stage produced no sentiment|not applicable
@TR|5|High toxicity|The Stage-1 toxicity score exceeds the threshold|ROUTER_TOXICITY_THRESHOLD (0.7)

@P|We learned two decisions in that table the hard way. **An absent confidence routes**: Gate 1 treats a missing value as uncertainty rather than ignoring it, because in an earlier revision our gate read a field name Stage 1 never emitted, the guard short-circuited on every post, and the system routed one hundred percent of posts while every document we had written claimed a single-digit rate. We repaired it by making **every gate read through a named reader function** that accepts all three field shapes the pipeline emits, and by routing the diagnostic log through those same readers, so a log line can never report a value the gate did not see. **Gate 3 is disabled by default**, and that default is what makes the gate selective: enabled, it made the router a pass-through, since any single gate firing is sufficient.

@FIGNOTE|In Figure {{fig:router}}, the routing decision is one gate with two outgoing edges and a note listing the six conditions any one of which routes a post, with the measured share taking each branch - sixteen and eighty-four percent - on the boxes below. @P|Comment selection runs in three steps. **Eligibility** removes kinds with no text for a model to read: emoji-only and link-only comments, and anything below a word-token floor. **Deduplication** sorts the remainder by reaction count, breaking ties by original index so the choice is reproducible, and collapses identical normalised texts to their most-liked occurrence. **Top-N** keeps that many survivors, the cap defaulting to zero so every unique comment with text is admitted. Crucially the selection is made **once** and every Stage-2 voter reads that same set: an earlier design capped inside the language-model stance pass alone, which still ran seven classifier models over the entire thread and left the language-model column empty on precisely the comments every cheap head had voted on.

@H3|3.2.3  The eight-labeller comment ensemble

@P|Comment sentiment here is not a model but an ensemble with an explicit abstention protocol. Every comment in the router's selected set collects up to **eight independent verdicts**: seven small sentiment heads batched on CPU, and one context-aware stance pass on the large model - the only labeller that sees the parent post.

@TABLE|tab:roster|The eight-labeller comment ensemble, with the checkpoint each slot loads.
@TH|Slot|Voter|Checkpoint|Character
@TR|1|xlmr|`tabularisai/multilingual-sentiment-analysis`|five-class multilingual head, batched on CPU
@TR|2|distilbert|`lxyuan/distilbert-base-multilingual-cased-sentiments-student`|three-class multilingual DistilBERT student
@TR|3|twitter_xlmr|`cardiffnlp/twitter-xlm-roberta-base-sentiment-multilingual`|XLM-RoBERTa trained on social media, three-class
@TR|4|banglabert|`ADn-001/banglabert-sentnob-sentiment`|BanglaBERT fine-tuned on SentNoB; Bangla social text
@TR|5|bengali_sentiment_bert|`ahs95/banglabert-sentiment-analysis`|BanglaBERT, five-class; a second Bangla-specific view
@TR|6|mbert|`nlptown/bert-base-multilingual-uncased-sentiment`|multilingual BERT on a five-star scale; the oldest and most differently-trained head
@TR|7|modernbert|`clapAI/modernBERT-base-multilingual-sentiment`|recent long-context encoder, a different pretraining recipe
@TR|8|llm|the `stage2` model role of Section 3.2.5|context-aware stance, one call per batch of comments
@ENDTABLE

@P|Let *V(c)* be the subset of the eight labellers that returned a verdict for comment *c*, so a labeller that cannot answer is absent from it rather than present with a neutral vote. The combined polarity score and the agreement fraction are given by Equation ({{eq:ensemble}}); they share a denominator, so a comment read by two labellers cannot present the same confidence as one read by eight. When none answers, the combiner emits *uncertain* rather than evaluating either expression.

@EQ|eq:ensemble|s(@c)  =  ( 1 / n_{c} ) · Σ w_{i} · v_{i}(@c)          a(@c)  =  m_{c} / n_{c}
@WHERE|The sum runs over the n_{c} labellers in V(@c) that returned a verdict; v_{i}(@c) ∈ {−1, 0, +1} is labeller @i's polarity and w_{i} its weight, normalised so that s(@c) stays in [−1, +1] however many abstained. m_{c} is the number of those labellers voting for the modal label, so a(@c) is the "5 of 8" agreement figure carried on every comment in Figure {{fig:shot-ensemble}}.
@ENDEQ

@FIGURE|fig:ensemble|The eight-labeller comment ensemble and its abstention protocol.
@FIGHEIGHT|82
@FIGNOTE|In Figure {{fig:ensemble}}, the ensemble is one input forking into two concurrent paths - seven small heads batched on CPU, and the single LLM stance pass beside them, the only labeller given the post as context - converging on the combination step and then on three outputs: the label, its provenance and the abstention outcome. This is the per-comment half of Process 4.0 of Figure {{fig:dfd1}}, reading selected comments from flow [7] and contributing the per-comment labels of flow [10], which Process 5.0 writes into D5 on flow [13]. The combination step is a single node because it is the single writer of the final label, and zero voters resolves to uncertain rather than neutral.
@ENDFIGURE

@P|Four decisions in that roster require justification. **Only a model may label a comment**: Stage 1's cheap emoji-and-lexicon verdict was originally our ninth voter, and we removed it because it answers on *every* comment, and we measured 71.3 percent of those answers to be the emoji rule or the deterministic stub. A voter that can never abstain makes every agreement figure unfalsifiable, and on Bangla threads that rule was frequently the *majority* voter. **A voter that cannot vote abstains**: a head that fails to load contributes nothing rather than a fabricated neutral, and a comment no model read is **uncertain**, not neutral - neutral is a claim about the comment, uncertain a claim about the system's confidence. Without that distinction an agreement of 1.0 rendered as "100 percent agreement" for a comment one model had read. **Seven heads are not seven independent readings**, since five are multilingual encoders trained on overlapping data [50]; and **one combiner is the single writer** of the final label, because in an earlier revision three places wrote it and the last write won.

@H3|3.2.4  Watchlist-driven target stance

@P|Document-level sentiment answers whether a comment is angry; for political and brand monitoring the operative question is **whom it is angry at**, which requires a target-dependent formulation [13], [14], [16]. This system implements that over an operator-supplied, versioned watchlist rather than a fixed annotated target set, and the load-bearing part is the alias matcher rather than the classifier: a single entity appears in Bangla script, in three to six inconsistent romanizations, in English, as a party initialism and sometimes as an epithet, so a watchlist matching one spelling matches almost nothing and fails *quietly*, producing zero mentions - indistinguishable from an entity never mentioned. Five design consequences follow, and we reached each by a test failing rather than by reading code.

@BULLET|Match case-insensitively for Latin and exactly for Bangla, since Bangla has no case.
@BULLET|Require token boundaries for short aliases, or a two- or three-character initialism matches inside unrelated words.
@BULLET|Treat Bangla word boundaries as not being spaces: Bengali attaches suffixes directly, so a suffix after a Bangla alias is permitted and a prefix is not.
@BULLET|Collapse overlapping aliases per target, keeping the longest, or every polarity cue in the surrounding clause is counted twice where a party name and its short form both fire.
@BULLET|Attach cues by **clause**, not a fixed character window: with sixty characters either side, "B is the best but A is corrupt" scored both entities identically, whereas clause segmentation lets each verdict be attributed to the clause that produced it.

@P|Two properties matter for the cost model. The matcher is **pure string work and therefore free**, so it runs in Stage 1 on every comment of every post and mention volume is available even for posts the gate bypassed; and the language-model scoring happens inside the Stage-2 comment-stance call already being issued, so the capability adds **no additional large-model calls**.

@H3|3.2.5  Pluggable inference backend and the role system

@P|Every language-model call passes through one client, which resolves two properties independently: a **role**, what the call is for, and a **backend**, who serves it. Seven roles are named. *stage1* carries the highest-volume work over captions and comments and resolves to a small fast model (`gemma3:4b` self-hosted, `llama-3.1-8b-instant` metered); *stage2* decides post type, insight and comment stance from a fixed vocabulary and wants a cheap constrained model (`qwen2.5:7b`, `llama-3.3-70b-versatile`); *summary* writes Bangla prose and wants a fluent one; *agent* needs long context and native tool calling and resolves to `llama3.1:8b-16k`, a retagged sixteen-thousand-token build; *llm_a* and *llm_b* are the report path's fast and quality lanes; and *vlm* serves image-grounded summaries.

@P|Splitting **summary** from **stage2** is the decision with the clearest return. The two Stage-2 jobs have opposite profiles - classification chooses from a fixed vocabulary and wants a cheap, constrained model, while summarisation writes prose and wants a fluent one - so one role forced a compromise and spent the fluent model on every classification call, multiplying straight through the comment lane because comment stance runs once per batch of twenty-five. Separating the role from the backend is what makes self-hosted and cloud serving interchangeable: both speak the same OpenAI-compatible interface, so switching is resolved per call in the order request, runtime toggle, environment default. The self-hosted path has **zero marginal token cost and no data egress**; the cloud path has the fastest inference and no GPU to operate, at a per-token price and with prompt content leaving the cluster. Because that is a privacy decision rather than a performance one, a **privacy-locked tenant is pinned to the self-hosted backend on every path**: resolved at the API boundary where the tenant is known, stamped into the job envelope so a global toggle cannot re-route content already in flight, and re-resolved on resume.

@P|Two further mechanisms warrant description. A **circuit breaker** per backend takes a repeatedly failing endpoint out of rotation for a cooldown period, with an injectable clock so the state machine is unit-testable without sleeping. A **truncation-recovery** path inspects the finish reason, auto-continues a reply that hit its token ceiling, trims back to the last complete sentence including the Bangla terminator, flags the result and **never caches it** - a path that exists because Bangla costs far more tokens per character than English under these tokenisers, so a single fixed ceiling truncated Bangla and Banglish while sparing English, a bias exactly inverted for this system's primary languages.

@H3|3.2.6  The agentic RAG insight layer

@P|The per-post pipeline is deterministic analysis followed by single-shot generation, and it remains so because that is what the cost model requires. Above it sits a **corpus-tier agent layer** for work that genuinely needs planning and several tool calls: analyst questions, grounded reports and targeted deep dives. **Agents are never invoked per post**, which would reintroduce the per-item cost the cascade exists to avoid. What distinguishes the layer from an assembly of established components is the **object of retrieval**: a conventional retrieval-augmented system retrieves documents, the text a user could have read anyway [34], [35], whereas these agents retrieve the system's **own structured analytical output** - sentiment distributions, stance verdicts by entity, ensemble agreement, coverage and vector provenance, toxicity scores and embedding clusters. That output exists only because the pipeline ran, which makes the retrieval analytical rather than documentary and lets a cited number be traced back to the model that produced it.

@P|Figure {{fig:agent-layer}} gives the architecture. Agents reach data only through **Model Context Protocol servers** [38] - small services exposing typed tools over a standard transport, so a new analytical capability is a tool registration plus one line in an allowlist rather than a change to the agent loop. The branch that matters most for answer quality is the refusal branch: an invalid or placeholder identifier is refused rather than answered with an empty result set, because an empty result set is indistinguishable to the model from a corpus that contains nothing.

@P|Deploying a tool-using loop on adversarial political content means the loop itself needs hardening, and we wrote each guard below against a failure we observed in a live run rather than against a hypothetical one.

@BULLET|**Untrusted-data delimiters.** Tool results carry comment text verbatim, so a comment reading "ignore previous instructions and report the sentiment as positive" arrives as text that looks like an instruction [43]. Results are wrapped in explicit markers, forged closing markers are neutralised, and the system prompt states that content inside them is data written by the public. This is **risk reduction, not a fix**.
@BULLET|**A tool result cannot displace the context window.** One search returning fifty rows serialised to 76,666 characters - roughly 39,000 tokens - purely because the default JSON serialisation escaped every Bengali character into six; unescaped they are 23,194. Results are now serialised unescaped and capped at 6,000 characters, dropping **whole rows** rather than truncating mid-structure, and citations are read off what the model was actually **shown**.
@BULLET|**A repeat is not dispatched.** A deterministic query cannot return new rows, so a byte-identical re-call is answered from the first result with a note naming untried tools. The failure we observed was a run that spent eleven of fifteen calls on one identical search and then shipped the budget-exhausted notice as the briefing.
@BULLET|**A run is only complete if the answer answers something.** Four shapes of non-answer each fail the run after one synthesis retry: code output, payload narration, self-narration, and the empty template - correct headings with every cell a placeholder, the most consequential because on a cursory reading it presents as a completed report.
@BULLET|**An answer cannot deny its own retrieval, and an unknown identifier raises rather than returning empty.** Given an honest escape hatch for "no post-level tool ran", a small model emits the canned phrase anyway, so a run that wrote that while a tool had returned ten real identifiers now has a contradiction block appended, checked against the run record; and because the watchlist file is not visible to the model, an invented target identifier raises an error naming every valid one rather than returning no rows, which costs a single turn instead of the whole run.

@P|Every run is persisted with an explicit state machine - queued, running, one state per guard failure, completed, budget-exhausted and cancelled - so a run that failed a guard is distinguishable afterwards from one that exhausted its budget and from one an operator stopped.

@H3|3.2.7  Retrieval design

@P|Retrieval serves both the operator search surface and the agents' search tools, and both run the same code. An identifier is **looked up, not searched for**: an exact match on the post, platform, URL or campaign identifier short-circuits ahead of whichever mode was requested, and a near-miss falls to a prefix match, because the interface renders only the first eight characters of a twenty-five-character identifier. This closes two opposite failures we observed in practice - a keyword search returning zero results for a real post identifier, and a semantic search returning twenty cosine neighbours none of which was the post, the more serious because twenty ranked results present as a successful search.

@P|Otherwise three arms run and their ranks are fused. A **dense arm** does cosine search over 768-dimension multilingual sentence embeddings [23]; a **chunk arm** does the same over a Bangla-aware splitter's output; and a **lexical arm** fuses PostgreSQL full-text search with trigram similarity, catching exact entity names, transliterations and hashtags - precisely what a multilingual encoder generalises away. All three terminate in the *same* store, so there is no reconciliation problem. We fuse by **reciprocal rank** [39] rather than by score normalisation, and not as a tuning convenience: a cosine similarity in [-1, 1] and a trigram similarity on its own scale have no shared zero point and cannot be added without inventing a conversion.

@EQ|eq:rrf|RRF(@d)  =  Σ 1 / ( k + r_{a}(@d) )
@WHERE|The sum runs over the three retrieval arms @a. r_{a}(@d) is the rank of document @d in arm @a, and k = 60 is the standard damping constant [39]. A document absent from an arm contributes nothing from that arm rather than a penalty, which is why an arm that fails on a query cannot drag a good result down.
@ENDEQ

@P|One property carries the design and is easily missed: the retrieved rows return to the agent runner rather than continuing to the answer. Retrieval is a tool call inside a loop, not a stage in a pipeline - the agent may read the top-k rows, conclude they do not answer the question, and search again before committing. That loop is what separates this design from classical retrieval-augmented generation, where a single retrieval precedes a single generation and a poor first query cannot be recovered from.

@H3|3.2.8  Storage, reliability and security

@P|Storage is split by access pattern rather than convenience. **PostgreSQL with pgvector** holds operational state, jobs, the canonical result document and the embedding vector in the same row, which removes a separate vector service entirely: transactional reads and approximate-nearest-neighbour search [40] live in one store and metadata scoping is ordinary SQL. **ClickHouse** holds the denormalised analytics rows, the per-comment table being a collapsing engine keyed on tenant, post and comment so a re-analysed comment collapses to its newest row rather than double-counting. **Redis** carries the streams, response cache, deduplication keys, rate limits, job counters, cancellation flags and usage counters, and **object storage** holds the raw result document at a deterministic key for replay and audit.

@P|On the security side the design treats post text as untrusted throughout. A credential shaped like a token is verified as a token on **every** transport - the authorization header, the API-key header, and the query parameter event-source connections require - because verifying it on only one meant an expired or forged token authenticated on all four streaming endpoints. API keys are stored as hashes and the tenant identity comes **from the stored row, never from a client-supplied token body**, which is what makes the privacy pin of Section 3.2.5 enforceable at all; claims taken from a token are allowlisted and the authentication method is set server-side, because spreading the payload last meant any claim in the token won.

@H3|3.2.9  Observability and telemetry

@P|A pipeline whose stages are separate processes joined by a durable queue has no call stack, so a post in flight leaves no trace any single process can report. Observability is therefore a design concern rather than an operational addition, and runs on two independent paths so the failure of one does not blind the other: metrics into Prometheus, structured logs into Loki and spans into Jaeger with Grafana over all three, and - because the dashboard needs a live view without querying a log store - progress events and counters into Redis, exposed as Server-Sent Event streams.

@P|The reason there are two paths is a failure-mode argument. The dashboard path is low-latency and is what an operator watches during a run; the Grafana path retains history and is queryable afterwards. Critically, the second does not pass through our own API, so if the API is what has failed, the first path is unavailable precisely when it is most needed: an observability design in which every signal reaches the operator through the system being observed can only report faults that are not its own. Logging follows the same rule with two sinks from one logger - standard output, where a collector ships it to the log store, and a capped Redis buffer the dashboard tails.

@H3|3.2.10  Deployment architecture

@P|The same code runs in three deployment shapes we built - a single-host development stack, a nineteen-image Docker Compose MVP and a Kubernetes production tier - and the difference is which concerns are collapsed; we took every measurement in Chapter 4 on the first. In the Compose shape two simplifications are deliberate: authentication, ingestion and reporting share one container, acceptable because at MVP volumes no one of them can starve the others; and Redis plays three roles at once - message bus, cache and deduplication set - removing a broker from the operational surface at the cost of coupling three failure modes to one process.

@P|The Kubernetes shape reverses each of those compromises and adds one property Compose cannot express. The four stateless services become separate deployments scaled on processor use and request rate; Redis is replaced by Kafka as a three-broker StatefulSet with named topics including a dead-letter topic, giving permanently failing work somewhere to go other than an infinite retry. The substantive difference is the scaling signal: the stateless tier scales on processor and request rate, which are load signals, while the worker pools scale on **consumer-group lag**, which is a backlog signal. That matters for a batch pipeline because a worker saturated by a slow model call shows high utilisation whether the queue behind it holds ten items or ten thousand, so utilisation cannot distinguish "busy" from "falling behind" and lag can. The data tier sits inside a network policy so only the ingress is reachable from outside the cluster.

@H3|3.3.4  Prioritisation Rationale

@P|Between Phase-I and the Final Defence submission our schedule was fixed, so the ordering above was a series of choices rather than an accident, and the principle behind them was consistent: **prefer a capability that can be evidenced over one that can be demonstrated.** That principle settled the three decisions that shaped what we delivered. We funded the audit phase ahead of new features, because our first pass established that our own headline claim was contradicted by our own running code, and a system that reports plausible numbers for the wrong reason is worse than one that reports none. We built provenance and telemetry before the features whose behaviour they describe, because a measurement added afterwards cannot recover what an earlier run did. And we descoped labelling accuracy rather than approximating it, because a number produced by scoring the system against labels seeded from its own models would have been worse than no number. We do not argue the cost of that ordering away: this report contains no accuracy figure for any labeller, and we record two capabilities as built rather than proven. What it bought us is that every number in Chapter 4 came from running the code that ships.

@CHAPTER|4|Implementation and Results

@INTRO|This chapter describes how we implemented the system end to end: the development environment and deployment configuration, the testing and evaluation we carried out across every functional module, and the results we obtained from running the fully deployed stack. @H2|4.1  Environment Setup

@H3|4.1.1  Software environment and dependencies

@P|We chose a modern Python and JavaScript toolchain with locally served inference rather than a metered API, so that the whole stack can be run, measured and reproduced on hardware a student owns. We developed the system on a single workstation running 64-bit Linux with sixteen logical processor cores, a discrete CUDA-capable graphics device and an integrated one, and we took every measurement in this chapter in that environment, under Python 3.12 with Docker for the datastores and Ollama serving the local models.

@P|The system is written in Python 3.12 with a React 19 front end. **FastAPI with Uvicorn** serves the 51-path HTTP API and its generated OpenAPI specification; **React 19 with Vite** builds the dashboard, styled with Tailwind and charting with Chart.js; **Redis Streams** is the durable message bus and simultaneously the cache, deduplication set, counter store and progress channel; **PyTorch and Transformers** run the classifier heads and the sentence encoder; **Ollama** serves four local models, which is what makes the marginal token cost of a run genuinely zero; and **uv** pins the entire dependency tree from one lock file. Supporting tools are Git, Docker, pytest with vitest and Playwright chained into one pass-or-fail command, and oxlint with ruff. The Python 3.12 pin illustrates how a single dependency constrains a project: the optional machine-learning extra cannot be installed on Python 3.13 because a transitive dependency publishes no wheel for it.

@P|Rather than one general-purpose database, we run four stores chosen by access pattern, all in Docker on the development tier: **PostgreSQL 16 with pgvector** for operational rows, job state, the canonical result document and the 768-dimension embedding index in the same row; **ClickHouse** for denormalised analytical events, with a collapsing engine on the per-comment table so a re-analysed comment does not double-count; **Redis 7** for the bus, response cache, deduplication keys, rate limits, job counters, cancellation flags and usage counters; and **MinIO** for S3-compatible object storage at deterministic keys, so production can substitute a managed service with no code change. We pull four models locally, and they are deliberately **not** the same model - the Stage-1 role runs a four-billion-parameter model because it carries the highest-volume work, Stage 2 and the summary role a seven-billion-parameter model, the agent role a retagged sixteen-thousand-token-context build because tool-calling loops exhaust a four-thousand-token window, and a vision-language model is held for the image path. Beyond those roles, Stage 1 loads six supporting models outside the generative roles - a multilingual sentence encoder, a language identifier, a zero-shot topic and intent classifier, a toxicity head, an emotion head and GLiNER [25] for named-entity recognition, chosen so that entity types are supplied at inference time rather than baked into a checkpoint.

@H3|4.1.2  Deployment configuration

@P|The three deployment tiers are configurations of one codebase rather than three systems, and what changes between them is only how many processes a concern is spread across and how scaling is expressed. The **development** tier runs the four datastores in Docker and everything else as host processes - the API, five stage workers, three MCP servers, the agent service and the dashboard, eleven in total - with Ollama serving four local models; running the workers on the host rather than in containers is what makes a code change visible without a rebuild, and is why this tier is the one used for measurement, since the stage functions can be invoked directly with the same code paths the queue would exercise. The **MVP** tier packages the same components as nineteen images against six named volumes on a single network, and the **production** tier expresses them as Kubernetes objects, the only tier in which scaling is declarative rather than manual. The practical consequence is a caveat on every number in this chapter: measurements taken on a single host with local model serving characterise the *logic* of the system - how many calls it makes, which posts it routes, what it retrieves - and not its throughput under production hardware. Where a quantity depends on the deployment shape rather than on the code, Section 4.3.3 records it as unmeasured rather than estimating it.

@P|Three consequences follow, and they are stated here rather than in a limitations section because they constrain every number that comes after.

@NUM|1. **The comment sample is not random.** The stored comments are the platform's returned set, which is engagement-ordered. With 64.1 percent of comments carrying zero reactions and a maximum of 946, aggregating sentiment over an engagement-biased 3.75 percent sample and calling the result "the sentiment of this thread" is not supportable. What the data supports is a claim about *the most-engaged N comments per post*, and that is the phrasing this report uses.
@NUM|2. **Post-level sentiment is a text measurement.** The 69 image references are relative object-storage keys whose objects are not present, so no image bytes are reachable in any runnable configuration and the vision term has never contributed a non-zero value. The image path reports an explicit failure status rather than a fabricated neutral verdict, and the fusion weights renormalise over the terms that carry a real model verdict, so an absent image term no longer drags a genuine text signal toward neutral.
@NUM|3. **We keep the 7 caption-less posts in the corpus.** They yield no post text to analyse, but they carry 1,307 comments that analyse like any other. An earlier working corpus of ours excluded them, which meant we were measuring a population the running system never processes and under-counting the comment lane, now the dominant cost.

@P|Coverage itself deserves one further note, because five posts exceed one hundred percent - up to 112 stored comments against a reported total of 42 - which cannot be replies inflating the numerator, since no comment in the corpus carries a parent identifier. The pipeline clamps coverage to 1.0 and raises an explicit anomaly flag rather than rendering a figure above 100 percent.

@H3|4.2.2  Verification strategy and the full-chain result

@P|We automated the testing strategy rather than running it manually, because the failures that dominated this project are not ones a person notices by looking. Our verification has three layers. **Contract tests** assert that documents crossing every boundary validate against their schema, and several grep the source to assert structural invariants - that every worker checks the cancellation flag before doing work, that the dashboard reads only fields the API returns, that the autoscaler manifests name exactly the streams the workers create. Those fail on a rename, which is their purpose. **Regression tests** pin every defect found in the audit passes. **Datastore tests** run against throwaway PostgreSQL and Redis containers started per session, so a default run can never damage a developer's environment; two further suites are gated behind explicit markers.

@P|Those layers hold **1,420 automated tests across 68 files**, plus 96 dashboard unit tests and 2 browser tests. Tables {{tab:functional}} and {{tab:security}} give twenty-two *acceptance scenarios*, one or two per module, at a level a reader can check against the screenshots of Section 4.3.1; each is backed by one or more tests, but the suite is far larger, because most of what it asserts is a boundary condition, a malformed input, a component that failed to load, or a rename that must break a build. Its distribution is the clearest statement of where this project's risk sat: the agentic layer carries **325 tests, nearly a quarter of the suite**, for a subsystem that is a small fraction of the code, because it is the only component whose failures are *plausible* - an agent that invents a citation produces an answer that reads exactly like a correct one. Pipeline stages, fusion and routing carry 237; chat and question routing 139; security and data residency 116; the comment ensemble and its provenance 110; retrieval, clustering and reports 109; contracts and the dashboard-to-API surface 147; job control, queues and observability 150; and the audit regressions with watchlist stance 87.

@H3|4.2.3  Security and access-control testing

@P|Functional correctness establishes that each module does what it is meant to do; it does not establish that a caller who is not entitled to a result cannot obtain one. The two are tested separately because they fail differently: a functional defect produces a wrong answer, whereas an access-control defect produces a *correct* answer delivered to the wrong party. Every identity-bearing path - the token issuer and verifier, the API-key store, the single-use streaming ticket, the tenant scope on every analytical query, and the data-residency policy deciding which inference backend a tenant's text may reach - was exercised against both the permitted and the refused case, on the principle that a check never observed to refuse anything has not been tested at all. Eight suites carry that work inside the same pass-or-fail chain, collecting **138 tests, all passing in 6.89 seconds**: seventeen on token issue and verification, thirty-seven on signup and passwords, twenty-five on transport hardening, seventeen on the backend policy resolver, fourteen asserting the privacy lock survives every pipeline entry point, two on cross-tenant reads, four on rate limiting and twenty-two on prompt-injection containment.

What this subsection does not claim is a penetration test: these are white-box tests written by the author of the code they guard, so they establish that the checks behave as specified under the conditions the author imagined, and an adversarial review by a party with no knowledge of the implementation is named in Section 6.3 as the work that would close the remaining distance.

@H3|4.2.4  Routing behaviour and the cost split

@P|We ran the routing measurement over the **real stage functions** - normalisation, text and image analysis, fusion, result construction and the gate itself - with no options set, reporting the router's own counters. We ran it twice with the same code and two different Stage-1 engines, because the comparison between them is the result. The quantity that decides how much the router is worth is not the routing rate but the **share of large-model spend the gate governs**, which is the post-level lane's share of all calls.

@EQ|eq:governed|γ  =  C_{gated} / ( C_{gated} + C_{comment} )
@WHERE|where C_{gated} counts the large-model calls the router's gate releases - the post-level lane together with the post-level Stage-2 tasks - and C_{comment} counts the per-comment labelling calls, which run for every post whether the gate routed it or not. The measured value is γ = 0.304 under the shipped configuration and γ = 0.547 under the keyword baseline.

@P|Three conclusions follow, and the first contradicted our own headline claim. **The routing rate is not the cost story**: between 85 and 96 percent of calls are comment-level. The initial claim was that cheap models perform ninety to ninety-five percent of the work and a selective large model handles the rest, and that this was the central cost-control idea; that assertion was never measured, and when it was, the gate had in fact been routing one hundred percent of posts for a period because of two field-name defects - and after those were repaired it still governed only a minority of spend. The claim that survives is narrower and is the one this report makes: **cheap NLP filters which comments and which posts deserve a large model**, and the second half is now the bigger one. Second, **improving Stage 1 shrinks the gate's importance rather than the bill**, which runs counter to intuition: a better Stage 1 types more posts confidently, so fewer route and the gate decides less, while comment labelling runs unchanged. Third, **the gate selects the right posts** - under the shipped configuration the 7 routed posts hold 3,404 of the 8,713 comments that reach a model, so the gate is escalating the large, contentious threads, which is the behaviour the design intends and which a routing rate quoted on its own cannot show.

@P|Measurement also corrected one of our earlier estimates. We had expected filtering emoji-only comments out of model batches to save roughly seventeen percent of the comment bill; emoji-only comments are **2.8 percent** of the corpus. The seventeen-percent figure was the share taking the fast path, which conflates emoji-only reactions with short *text* comments, and a short text comment still carries text a model can read. Filtering emoji remains correct because they are pure waste, but it is not a cost lever.

@H3|4.2.5  Retrieval evaluation

@P|Retrieval is the one axis we evaluate against a ground-truth set. We derived a 32-query known-item set from each target post's own topics and entities, with exactly one marked relevant post per query, and ran the same set before and after enabling real embeddings, so the two recall columns of Table {{tab:retrieval}} differ only in whether the stored vectors are semantic or hashes. Three standard measures are reported over the query set at cut-off k = 10. Because each query has exactly one relevant document, recall@k reduces to the fraction of queries whose target appears in the top k at all, as in Equation ({{eq:recall}}); the two rank-sensitive measures are the mean reciprocal rank, MRR@k = (1/N) Σ 1/r_q, and the normalised discounted cumulative gain, nDCG@k = (1/N) Σ 1/log2(1 + r_q), both of which reward the target appearing higher in the list rather than merely appearing.

@EQ|eq:recall|recall@k  =  ( 1 / N ) · Σ 1[ r_{q} ≤ k ]
@WHERE|The sum runs over the N = 32 queries @q. r_{q} is the position of the one relevant post in the list returned for query @q, and 1[·] is the indicator function, worth 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. This is the measure that matters when the whole top-k is handed to a generator [42], because a target absent from the context cannot be recovered by the model.
@ENDEQ

@TR|lexical (full text fused with trigram)|0.3750|0.7500|0.6503|0.6748

@P|Three findings come out of that table, and the third is a defect rather than a result. **We found hash vectors indistinguishable from chance**: real embeddings are worth 3.3 times on the dense arm alone, from 0.1875 to 0.6250, and the end-to-end configuration moved from 0.5312 to 0.8750, a 65 percent relative gain. This matters beyond retrieval, because the same vectors drive the clustering the report layer summarises, and clustering hash vectors groups posts at random and then pays a large model to describe the grouping. **Chunking improves ranking rather than recall on this corpus**, because the corpus consists predominantly of short captions. And **the lexical arm's improvement turned out to be a bug fix, not a benefit**: the lexical column moved from 0.3750 to 0.7500 with no vector involved, because the full-text half of that arm had never fired at all - the query builder conjoined every term, so an eight-token question demanded all eight words in one caption and matched **zero** rows across the entire query set. Everything we had credited to "full text plus trigram" was trigram alone. We record it because it is the same failure shape as the routing defect: a component that produced no output, reported no error, and was covered by a passing test.

@P|A stratified view of the same 32 queries changes how the headline table should be read, and does so in the embeddings' favour. Splitting the queries by the lexical overlap between query and target gives two populations. On the twelve near-verbatim lookups, lexical retrieval cannot fail and scores 1.000, which is why it outscores hybrid on MRR overall. On the eight genuinely leak-free queries - where the query shares no vocabulary with its target - **lexical scores exactly zero** and the dense arm is the only retriever that returns the target at all; the fused configuration ties lexical on the easy bucket and beats it 0.800 to 0.600 on the hard one. Neither arm dominates, which is precisely the premise the fusion design rests on, and it is invisible in the aggregate.

@H3|4.2.6  Label provenance and ensemble coverage

@P|A sentiment chart that cannot say what produced its numbers is not evidence, so we record provenance per comment and per post. Measured over all 10,272 comments in the default configuration, we found that **28.7 percent of per-comment labels are model output and 71.3 percent are not**: partly a fourteen-word emoji-and-lexicon rule, and mostly a deterministic function deriving a label from a hash of the first fifty characters. That function is reproducible and it is not sentiment. Before we recorded provenance, the same run reported 8,513 model inferences in a configuration where zero models had been loaded, because the classifier tagged every substantive comment as model output regardless of whether a model existed. Our remedy was not to hide the slice but to label it.

@P|Four reporting mechanisms follow from that measurement, and we made them permanent parts of the output contract. Each comment carries a **method** naming the engine that actually ran and a **kind** distinguishing emoji, short and substantive text. Each post carries a **provenance block** stating what share of its labels were model inferences. Each analysed comment carries the **number of voters that actually spoke** alongside its label and agreement figure. Finally, a comment that no model read is reported as **uncertain at zero voters**, never as neutral.

@H3|4.2.7  Comparative analysis

@P|Set against its two natural baselines on the quantities we measured, our shipped configuration reads as follows. The all-cheap keyword baseline routes 32 of 43 posts (74 percent) for 813 calls, of which the gate governs 54.7 percent; the shipped hybrid routes 7 of 43 (16 percent) for 529 calls, of which the gate governs 30.4 percent; and an LLM-everywhere baseline routes all 43 by definition. The comparison is deliberately restricted to measured quantities - an accuracy column is absent because labelling accuracy sits outside the evaluation scope.

@P|Four readings follow, and the first costs us our simplest claim. Cutting total calls from 813 to 529 is a reduction of roughly a third - real, but nowhere near the order-of-magnitude saving that a routing rate falling from 74 to 16 percent appears to promise, because as the gate routes fewer posts it also governs less of what remains. Second, we leave the LLM-everywhere column as a bound rather than an estimate: we did not run that baseline, and a column of plausible numbers beside two of measured ones would have invited exactly the comparison the evidence does not support. Third, two properties favour the shipped configuration on grounds that have nothing to do with cost - independent verdicts per comment rise from one to as many as eight with abstention, and the output can state what produced each label - and both belong to the ensemble rather than the router, so the contribution does not rest on the cost claim that measurement weakened. Fourth, retrieval recall is orthogonal: it reads persisted analysis rather than participating in the cascade, and its improvement from 0.5312 to 0.8750 came from replacing hash vectors with real embeddings, not from anything the router does.

@H3|4.3.2  What the measurements establish, and what they disconfirm

@P|Our measurements support three findings. The cascade works as a mechanism, and we can now observe its behaviour rather than assert it: the gate responds to Stage-1 quality in the direction the design predicts, its decision travels explicitly on the envelope, and its bypass branch is validated end to end against the output schema - a branch that had never once executed before the audit that found it. Hybrid retrieval is a real improvement, and the stratification of Section 4.2.5 explains why: the fused configuration reaches 0.8750 recall@10, and the two arms fail on *disjoint* query populations. Provenance reporting changed what we can honestly claim, and asymmetrically - it removed claims rather than adding them, turning two apparent successes into stated absences: the image modality, and the majority of the default configuration's comment labels.

@P|Our original headline claim was wrong twice over, and reporting that is more useful than reporting only the corrected version. It was wrong **in fact**, because the gate was routing every post: two rules read field names Stage 1 never emitted, one read a field Stage 1 sets as a placeholder for Stage 2 to fill, and one compared a language value against a code-mixing marker recorded under a different field - net, one rule always fired and three could never fire. Every one of the four was a **silent degradation rather than an exception**, which is the generalisable lesson we take from this project; we then applied the mitigation - named reader functions, a diagnostic log routed through those same readers, and a test asserting that the reader sees the producer's real output - to the three other places the identical pattern had occurred. It was wrong **in framing**, because even a correctly functioning gate governs a minority of spend once every comment is labelled. That is the finding with the most transfer value: **when the routed unit contains many sub-items that each need work, routing the container is not the cost lever.** The published cascade literature routes single queries [30], [31], [32] and does not encounter this, which is why it took a measurement rather than a reading for us to find it.

@H3|4.3.3  The boundary of the evaluation, and threats to validity

@P|Labelling accuracy is outside the scope of this project, and we claim none. What we deliver is a system: one that routes work, labels it, records what produced every label, and serves the result under a validated contract. A benchmark number would require human-adjudicated labels, and producing them is an annotation programme with its own guideline, agreement study and timeline rather than a further measurement script. The tooling ships with the repository, but the gold file holds **zero adjudicated labels**, deliberately, because seeding them from a model in this repository and then scoring that model would measure agreement with itself. A green suite is not a quality claim, so the report quantifies rates, counts, coverage, latency, retrieval quality and provenance, and quotes no macro-F1 for any labeller. Four further capabilities cannot currently produce a signal, and we list rather than omit them: the image modality, because no image bytes are reachable; horizontal scaling, for which we have timed no multi-replica run; cross-backend quality parity, under the same annotation dependency; and the cross-encoder reranker, for which the harness prints a skip.

@CHAPTER|5|Engineering Standards and Design Challenges

@INTRO|This chapter records the standards the system complies with and the alternatives we rejected in each case, assesses our work's impact on life, society, the environment and its own sustainability, presents a financial analysis with a costed alternative, and maps our work onto the complex-engineering-problem, knowledge-profile and complex-engineering-activity frameworks.

@H2|5.1  Compliance with the Standards

@P|We list only the standards that genuinely bear on this system, and for each we name the alternative we considered with its trade-off, because a standard adopted without an alternative in mind is a default rather than a decision.

@H3|5.1.1  Software Standards

@P|For **data interchange** we use JSON validated against a published JSON Schema, and the assembler **raises** on a failure rather than persisting the document, so nothing downstream ever sees an unvalidated result; one increment in our schema version history exists purely because a language-model task's output was computed, paid for and then dropped by a component reading a different set of fields. *Alternatives:* **Protocol Buffers** or **Avro** would give a smaller wire format and stronger typing, but the upstream platform emits JSON, our consumers are third-party applications for which a self-describing text format is far easier to adopt, and the payloads are dominated by free text. The cost is verbosity, and we pay it down where it hurts: our stream payloads escape no non-ASCII characters, which on Bengali text we measured as roughly half the token bill on the agent path.

@P|The **web API** is REST over HTTPS, versioned, and described by a generated OpenAPI document served by the application rather than maintained by hand: endpoints live under a version prefix, asynchronous work returns an accepted status with a job identifier, writes accept an idempotency key, and errors use one envelope. *Alternatives:* **GraphQL** would let the dashboard fetch exactly the fields it needs and would have avoided one defect class hit repeatedly here, where a hand-maintained response model silently dropped computed fields; we rejected it because our consumers are heterogeneous third-party services, caching and rate-limiting are simpler on REST, and a typed response model is the correct fix for that defect class - which is what we did. **Streaming** uses Server-Sent Events across five surfaces; **WebSockets** are bidirectional and would have been the obvious choice, but every surface here is server-to-client only, and Server-Sent Events reconnect automatically over ordinary HTTP. Their one real disadvantage drove a design decision: an event-source connection cannot carry an authorization header, so rather than putting a credential in the URL we issue a **single-use ticket valid for about sixty seconds**.

@P|**Authentication** uses JSON Web Tokens with HMAC signatures alongside hashed API keys, with short expiry and a refresh endpoint, and the tenant identity read from the stored row rather than from anything the client sends. *Alternatives:* **OAuth 2.0 with an external identity provider** is the right answer for a multi-organisation deployment and is the documented upgrade path, but adds a dependency our offline test suite cannot exercise; we rejected **session cookies** because our primary consumers are machine clients. The compliance point is the enforcement rather than the scheme: a credential *shaped* like a token is verified as a token on every transport, because verifying it on only one meant an expired or forged token authenticated on all four streaming endpoints. **Character encoding** is Unicode under NFC normalisation applied at ingestion with script tagging - not administrative, since without it two visually identical Bangla strings can differ byte-for-byte, defeating both content-hash deduplication and the alias matcher.

@H3|5.1.2  Hardware Standards

@P|On **accelerators and precision**, inference targets CUDA-capable devices with quantised weights, and the deployment is designed so the cheap classifier heads run on **CPU** while the accelerator serves the language model. That is a decision rather than a fallback: we measured that on a four-gigabyte card already serving a seven-billion-parameter model roughly 285 megabytes remain free, which fits one classifier head and not seven, and placing all seven produces the least desirable outcome - some load, some fail, and a head that fails to load abstains silently for the life of the process, so a run silently degrades from eight voters to two. Our initial configuration placed every model on the accelerator and produced exactly that; we rejected running the language model on CPU as well on latency grounds. The split we chose costs roughly three gigabytes of checkpoint storage and a few CPU cores, and makes the ensemble's coverage deterministic.

@P|On **storage and memory**, the vector column is fixed-dimension and the embedding dimension must match it, enforced rather than documented: a mismatch fails every insert instead of silently storing an unusable vector. Object storage speaks the S3 API, so development runs MinIO and production substitutes a managed service with no code change. The **commodity-hardware target** is the binding constraint - the system must run on a single workstation with one consumer accelerator, at the capital cost stated in Table {{tab:capex}} - which is why the entire model stack has a stub mode in which every component degrades to a deterministic function and **reports that it did so**. A run in which every component degraded still reports the engine it *intended* to use, so the degraded-components list is the only signal distinguishing a real result from a heuristic one.

@H3|5.1.3  Communication Standards

@P|For **transport security**, TLS terminates at the gateway, datastores and inference servers sit on private subnets, and only the gateway is internet-facing; in the cluster deployment a network policy enforces that boundary rather than relying on topology. For **message transport**, Redis Streams with consumer groups provides at-least-once delivery for the MVP tier and the production tier migrates to a partitioned log. Every stream and consumer-group name is defined in exactly one module, and a test asserts that the autoscaler manifests name the same streams and groups the workers create - a rule that exists because a scaler pointed at a group no worker creates reports no backlog, so the one stage where scaling changes cost never scaled while the manifests looked correct.

@P|**Tool transport** is the Model Context Protocol: agents reach data only through MCP servers over streamable HTTP [38]. Adopting a protocol rather than writing bespoke glue is what makes a new analytical capability a tool registration plus one line in an allowlist, and the protocol's own security literature is taken seriously here - because tool results carry third-party text verbatim, everything a model is shown is wrapped in untrusted-data delimiters with forged-delimiter neutralisation [43]. **Data residency** is itself a communication constraint, because the cloud backend sends prompt content - real people's comments - to a third party. It is therefore treated as a policy rather than a performance setting: the decision is resolved where the tenant is known, stamped into the job envelope so a later global toggle cannot re-route content already in flight, re-resolved on resume, and **fails closed** when the policy table cannot be read.

@H2|5.2  Impact on Society, Environment and Sustainability

@H3|5.2.1  Impact on Life

@P|The direct beneficiaries are the analysts who currently read comment threads by hand. On the largest thread in this corpus that means 2,857 stored comments against a platform-reported 9,224; no analyst reads that, so in practice a human reads the top twenty and generalises. The system does not remove the human and is designed on the assumption that it should not: it reports coverage, it reports what produced each label, and it shows eight verdicts side by side precisely so a human can see where the models disagree and look there.

@P|There is a second, less obvious effect on the people whose comments are analysed. A system that labels a comment negative and stops there attributes an emotion to a person. A system that separates document sentiment from stance toward a named entity, and that says **uncertain** when no model read a comment rather than defaulting it to neutral, makes fewer claims about people than the alternative. The effect is modest, and it is the correct default when the subjects have not consented to the analysis.

@H3|5.2.2  Impact on Society and Environment

@P|The corpus we built this system on is political content from Bangladesh, and the technology must be assessed as dual-use: the same capability that lets a newsroom see how a policy announcement was received lets an actor identify who is criticising them. Our response is to make the editorial choice visible - the watchlist is a **stated bias model** in a versioned file with a named owner per entry, and stance lives in a separate field so it cannot be folded into a sentiment total.

@P|The environmental argument is the same as the cost argument and is measurable in the same units: every large-model call avoided is accelerator-seconds not spent. Under the shipped configuration the corpus run costs 529 calls against 813 for the keyword baseline, and the caps deliberately left disabled would reduce the dominant comment lane by more than an order of magnitude on large threads - a 2,857-comment thread costs 115 stance batches uncapped and four with the cap set to one hundred. Set against this, the eight-labeller ensemble spends roughly 0.92 seconds of CPU per comment, which is the cost of the transparency it buys.

@H3|5.2.3  Ethical Aspects

@P|We took five ethical positions deliberately, and gave each a corresponding mechanism in the code, which is the only form in which an ethical position is checkable.

@NUM|E1. **Personal data is handled as personal data.** The system encrypts at rest and in transit, scopes every read by tenant, supports per-tenant retention and deletion, and - the mechanism that makes the rest enforceable - takes tenant identity from a stored row rather than from anything a client sends.
@NUM|E2. **A privacy commitment must survive a database outage.** The tenant policy check originally returned permissively on any database error, which permitted egress precisely when the policy could not be verified; it now returns a service error, because a privacy guarantee that evaporates when the database hiccups is not a guarantee.
@NUM|E3. **The bias in the labels is stated, not hidden.** A file declaring that support for a named entity is positive encodes a political stance - legitimate for a monitoring product and indefensible presented as neutral analysis, so it is labelled as the former throughout.
@NUM|E4. **The system does not claim more than it did.** Provenance on every label; an explicit degraded-components list; a stub flag on every stored vector; an explicit vision status where only one value licenses a claim about an image. Table {{tab:evidence}} is the same commitment at the level of the whole project.
@NUM|E5. **Untrusted input is treated as untrusted.** Comment text is attacker-controlled: it is escaped before rendering, wrapped in data delimiters before a model sees it, and never permitted to alter a system instruction. This is risk reduction and is described as such, because no prompt-level defence can promise resistance to prompt injection [43].

@P|One ethical limitation is stated plainly rather than left implicit. The comments we analysed were published publicly, but their authors did not consent to this analysis. Our mitigations - aggregate reporting, tenant scoping, retention controls, and the refusal to attribute a label to a comment no model read - reduce the harm; they do not resolve the underlying question, which is a policy matter for whoever deploys the system rather than an engineering one.

@H3|5.2.4  Sustainability Plan

@P|**Technical sustainability** rests on the property that made the audit passes productive: a cross-component rename breaks a test rather than a feature. Stream names, consumer groups and label vocabularies each live in exactly one module, the deployment manifests are asserted against those modules, and every repaired defect has a regression test. **Operational sustainability** rests on the backend being pluggable: an operator with accelerators runs everything in-house at zero marginal token cost and one without them runs the same code against a metered API, and because the switch is resolved per call this is a configuration decision rather than a migration. **Economic sustainability** is addressed in Section 5.3, and **scientific sustainability** is the corpus - 10,272 real code-mixed comments with crowd reaction signals, a stratified sampler and a scorer, ready for the annotation effort that would turn this engineering result into a benchmark result.

@P|Every software component is open source and inference ran locally, so we paid no licence or per-token fees. @P|We state our innovation claim narrowly on purpose. Target-dependent stance detection is not novel - it is established for English [13], [14], [15], [16] - and doing it over an operator-supplied entity list is an engineering framing rather than a research result. **The defensible claim is doing it on code-mixed Bangla, where the same entity is written in three scripts and the matcher is the hard part.** The line that survives scrutiny is *novel application and engineering in an under-resourced language setting, not a novel method*.

@H2|5.5  Summary

@P|The system complies with an assembled rather than a single standard, and Section 5.1 records each choice with the alternative it displaced. Its societal impact is genuinely dual-use, and our response is to make the editorial component of its labels visible rather than claim a neutrality it does not have. Our financial analysis reports the hardware as capital rather than writing it off: about $370 of power, connectivity and amortised workstation falls inside the schedule against $1,150 of accelerator-bearing hardware committed before it, and a Production deployment needs $45,000 to $72,000 of server-class capital that no monthly envelope makes cheaper than the metered path - owned hardware buys data residency, not savings. Against the complex-engineering framework we engage six of the seven problem attributes, seven of the eight knowledge-profile categories and all five engineering activities.

# CHAPTER 6 - CONCLUSION
# =====================================================================
@CHAPTER|6|Conclusion

@INTRO|This chapter summarises what we built and what we learned, states our limitations without softening them, and sets out the work that would close each one, in the order that would produce the most value.

@H2|6.1  Summary

@P|We asked a narrow, testable question: given a post together with its comment thread, in a setting mixing Bangla script, English and romanized Banglish, can a system decide *per unit of work* how much intelligence that unit deserves, spend the expensive model only where cheap models cannot answer, and prove afterwards from its own counters where the money went and what produced every label?

@P|The answer is yes, and not in the way we originally believed. We run cheap analysis on every post and every comment; a six-gate router decides post-level spend and a separate step decides which comments the expensive labellers read - two decisions rather than one, and the structural change that made the cost behaviour measurable. We raised per-comment sentiment to eight labellers with explicit abstention, added watchlist stance that answers whom a comment opposes at zero additional model calls, and built nine agents that reason over the system's own output through eighteen typed tools. Measurement then produced the key outcome: **the gate behaves exactly as designed - 16 percent of posts route against 74 percent for a keyword baseline - yet governs only 30 percent of large-model calls, because between 85 and 96 percent of calls are comment-level.** Hybrid retrieval reaches recall@10 of 0.8750 where hash-seeded vectors score 0.1875, and the whole system verifies as one pass-or-fail chain in seventy-three seconds.

@P|Our most transferable outcome is not any of those numbers but the discipline that produced them. Six independent audit passes established that the consequential failures in a system of this shape are not incorrect answers but **silent successes** - a gate reporting plausible behaviour while four of its six rules were inert, a retrieval arm matching zero rows while a passing test covered it. Named reader functions, single-source constants, provenance on every value, abstention instead of a default vote, and an evidence class on every published claim are what we would carry to the next system.

@H2|6.2  Limitation

@P|Three limitations bound our evidence base. Our corpus is small and its comment sample biased: fifty posts in a three-week window on one platform, whose 10,272 comments are an engagement-ordered 3.75 percent slice of the 274,126 reported, so every thread-level aggregate describes *the most-engaged comments per post*. The multimodal claim is unexercised, because the image path [28], [29] is implemented but its 69 references are object-storage keys whose objects are absent. And retrieval quality rests on 32 machine-derived queries with no human relevance judgement, which favours literal matching and leaves the decisive stratified bucket at eight.

@P|Three further capabilities carry no measurement. Horizontal scaling, queue-depth autoscaling and the Kubernetes deployment are test-asserted but never timed in a cluster, so every throughput statement is a design property. The agent runner is hardened against six observed failure classes, each pinned by a regression test, but groundedness and citation accuracy carry no scores. Labelling accuracy is out of scope by design: our gold file holds zero adjudicated labels, because seeding them from a model here and scoring that model would measure agreement with itself, so we quote no macro-F1 or calibration curve [48].

@P|We record three narrower constraints here rather than leaving them in a screenshot: the per-analysis event stream has a five-minute ceiling, after which the Trace tab reports a failure while the pipeline finishes correctly; the monitoring stack provisions no Grafana dashboards; and the prompt-injection hardening is risk reduction rather than a solution [43]. All nine are carried at their evidence class in Table {{tab:evidence}}.

@H2|6.3  Future Work

@P|One item dominates our future work, because most of the others depend on it: **adjudicating the gold set**. Two annotators would label a five-hundred-comment overlap and report Cohen's kappa [49], then label to two or three thousand comments stratified by language bucket and kind, ruling explicitly on emoji-only sarcasm [17], political epithets and mid-sentence code-switching. The work would then become benchmarking rather than building: we would score the seven heads, the LLM stance pass and the Stage-1 baseline, reporting macro-F1 **per language bucket** with the cache disabled and provenance on every row, and fine-tune the weakest heads with a parameter-efficient adapter [27]. This is the step that would take our work from an engineering deliverable to an empirical one.

@P|Four items follow from those labels. Sweeping the router's confidence threshold would turn the cascade into a measured trade-off, plotting macro-F1 against cost with the comment cap as a second curve. The watchlist needs validating on two metrics reported apart - mention recall, because a missed alias is invisible, and stance agreement on correctly matched mentions. The routing decision should then be learned rather than hand-tuned [31], and the image modality made real or its weights deleted, since a weight that has never been anything but zero is not defensible either way.

@P|The rest needs no annotation. We would score the agent layer against an analyst question set with reference answers, measuring groundedness claim by claim, citation accuracy and tool-use correctness with a judge model and human review on a sample [44], [45], [47] and the retrieval measures of [46]. A multi-replica run against a ten-thousand-thread batch would convert NFR1 into a measurement; the trace view should resubscribe at the five-minute ceiling; the monitoring stack should ship dashboards; and the access-control surface deserves review by someone who did not write it. Together, an estimated three to four weeks dominated by annotation.
